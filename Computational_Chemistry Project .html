Project objective

This digital lab demonstrates three connected levels of molecular modelling: a π-electron Hückel model for conjugated molecules, molecular mechanics for classical force-field energies, and quantum-chemical methods that describe electrons more explicitly. Model & interpretation

The H₂ Morse potential

V(r) = Dₑ [1 − e−a(r−rₑ)]2 − Dₑ

Dₑ is the well depth, rₑ is the equilibrium bond distance, and a controls the width of the energy well. Each atom pair has a distinct parameter set.

MinimumAt r = rₑ, the bond is at its equilibrium geometry and the energy is lowest.
StretchingMoving away from equilibrium raises the potential energy; the restoring force acts toward the minimum.
DissociationAt large separation, the energy approaches zero relative to the separated-atom reference.

Representative diatomic constants are converted into an effective Morse curve. D₀ is adjusted by an approximate zero-point correction to estimate Dₑ; the width a is derived from the harmonic force constant. The curve is illustrative, not a high-level potential-energy calculation.

What a force field contains

Energy is a sum of terms

Etotal = Ebond + Eangle + Etorsion + Enon-bonded

Bond stretching: energy rises when a bond length differs from its equilibrium value.

Angle bending: penalizes changes in the angle between connected bonds.

Torsional energy: describes rotation around a bond and the preferred conformations.

Non-bonded energy: commonly includes van der Waals and electrostatic interactions between atoms that are not directly bonded.

The sliders use a toy set of constants: harmonic bond and angle terms, a threefold cosine torsion, and a Lennard–Jones 12–6 term for the non-bonded slider. This toy non-bonded term omits electrostatics. Real force fields use atom types, fitted parameters, exclusions and more detailed functional forms; standard fixed-topology force fields generally do not describe bond breaking or electronic excitations.

03 / Electronic structure
Choose your
method.

Different computational methods make different approximations. Method choice depends on molecular size, the property of interest, desired accuracy and available computing resources.

Classical · fastest

Molecular mechanics

Uses parameterized force fields for molecular structures, conformations and many simulations of large systems.

Electrons are not treated explicitly; bond rearrangements are usually outside a standard fixed-topology force field.
π-electron model

Hückel theory

A simple approximation for conjugated systems. It helps explain π-orbital energies, electron filling and trends in conjugation.

Qualitative model; does not provide a complete all-electron description.
Wavefunction · mean field

Hartree–Fock

Approximates the many-electron wavefunction with a single Slater determinant and treats exchange exactly within that model.

Does not include the full electron-correlation energy.
Electron density

Density functional theory

Uses electron density and an approximate exchange-correlation functional. Often offers a practical balance of cost and accuracy.

Results depend on the chosen functional, basis set and system.
Post-Hartree–Fock

MP2

Adds a second-order perturbative treatment of electron correlation beyond Hartree–Fock.

More costly than Hartree–Fock and can be unreliable for some strongly correlated systems.
High-accuracy benchmark

CCSD(T)

Coupled-cluster theory with singles, doubles and perturbative triples is a widely used high-accuracy approach for suitable small systems.

Computationally expensive; cost grows steeply with system size.
Basis set

Mathematical functions used to represent molecular orbitals, such as 6-31G(d) or def2-TZVP.

Geometry optimisation

Searches for nuclear coordinates that minimise the calculated energy under the chosen model.

Electron correlation

The correlated motion of electrons beyond a simple independent-particle description.

The workflow.

A reliable computational study is more than pressing “calculate”. The model, settings and checks should match the chemical question.

Structure

02 — MODEL

Method + basis

Select a force field or quantum method and appropriate basis set.

03 — OPTIMISE

Geometry

Find a stationary molecular geometry and verify convergence.

04 — VERIFY

Frequencies

Check vibrational frequencies to identify a minimum or transition state.

05 — ANALYSE

Properties

Interpret energies, orbitals or spectra, and compare with trusted data.

Important check: a true minimum on a well-behaved potential energy surface has no imaginary vibrational frequencies. A first-order transition state typically has one imaginary frequency corresponding to the reaction coordinate.

Theory

Why color is hard to teach

Conjugation changes π-orbital energy spacings. This tool converts a simplified ground-state Hückel gap into a photon-wavelength equivalent and illustrates an approximate complementary hue; it does not calculate an experimental colour or absorption spectrum.

Hückel levels

Ignoring σ-electrons, a linear polyene with N carbons has π energies

E(j) = α + 2β cos( jπ / (N + 1) ), j = 1 … N

α is the common carbon 2p Coulomb integral and β is the resonance integral between neighbouring p orbitals. The plotted builder matrix uses α = 0 as a reference because a common α shift moves every level equally and cancels from ΔE; β is negative in the usual convention, so ψ₁ is lowest.

What the moving electron means

The moving dot is a schematic teaching cue for promotion from an occupied frontier orbital to an unoccupied orbital after absorption of suitable photon energy. It is not a literal electron trajectory or a time-dependent quantum simulation; this page does not calculate transition dipole moments or selection rules.

Gap and absorption

For an even, neutral linear chain with N = 2m π electrons (one per carbon), levels j = 1 through m are doubly occupied: the HOMO is j = m and the LUMO is j = m + 1. Odd-electron systems have a singly occupied molecular orbital (SOMO) and require open-shell interpretation. Longer ideal linear chains generally narrow the simple Hückel frontier-orbital gap, but this gap is not automatically the optical excitation energy.

What this model leaves out

This page uses a one-p-orbital Hückel model for selected all-carbon conjugated structures. The plotted gap is a ground-state orbital-energy difference; actual optical excitation energies also depend on electron–hole interaction, state character and selection rules. Quantitative molecular spectra normally require an excited-state method such as TD-DFT or an appropriate wavefunction approach, plus consideration of solvent and vibronic effects. The colour display is only a trend illustration.

How computational chemistry is used

A researcher begins with a chemical question, builds a molecular model, chooses a method, performs a calculation and checks whether the result is physically meaningful. A converged geometry optimisation locates a stationary point; a harmonic frequency analysis at the same level helps classify it as a minimum or a transition state.

Applications in everyday technology

Computational chemistry supports research on drug candidates, battery electrolytes, polymers, pigments, sunscreen ingredients and catalysts. It can help explain molecular interactions and narrow down experiments, but experimental validation remains essential.

From Hückel theory to computational chemistry

Hückel theory is one simplified electronic-structure model. Molecular mechanics uses classical force fields, while methods such as Hartree–Fock, density functional theory and coupled cluster treat electrons with different approximations.

Reference data used in the teaching curves

Representative diatomic bond lengths, dissociation energies and harmonic force constants were taken from standard spectroscopic/physical-chemistry tables. The Morse width parameter a is consistent with the near-equilibrium curvature k = 2Dₑa². When k is taken from tables in mdyn Å⁻¹, it must be converted to eV Å⁻² before using this relation with Dₑ in eV. These rounded values are for visual comparison, not a refit to experimental potential curves.
